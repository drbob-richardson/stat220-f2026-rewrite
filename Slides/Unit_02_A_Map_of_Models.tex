\section{Why}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{Where we left off}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Unit 1 compared two groups. One estimate, one standard error, one question.
  \item Almost nothing you will be handed at work looks like that. You get a table with forty
        columns and a vague question attached.
  \item This unit is the map: what is available, what each one lets you do, and how to pick.
\end{itemize}
\begin{principle}{the honest starting point}
``Which model should I use?'' has no answer until you say what you want out of it. Most bad
modeling starts with someone skipping that sentence.
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Every model is the same sentence}
\[
y \;=\; f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_p) \;+\; \text{error}
\]
\begin{itemize}
  \item $y$ is the thing you want to know. The $x$'s are what you already have.
  \item $f$ is the part the model supplies. Choosing a model is choosing what shapes $f$ is
        allowed to take.
  \item The error is everything $f$ does not account for. Some models make a claim about it.
        Others say nothing at all.
\end{itemize}
\begin{principle}{that is the whole menu}
Every family in this unit is a different answer to two questions. What is $f$ allowed to look
like, and do we say anything about the error?
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What this unit gives you}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
\textbf{Things to know}
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item the main model families, and what each one assumes
  \item that the type of $y$ decides most of the choice for you
  \item the five jobs people hand a model
  \item the five things you do to any model once you pick it
  \item how to measure error honestly, and compare two models
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
\textbf{Mistakes to stop making}
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item picking the algorithm before naming the goal
  \item forcing the wrong model onto the type of $y$
  \item judging a model on the data you fit it to
  \item reading a penalized coefficient as an effect
  \item reaching for flexibility on a few hundred rows
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{The menu}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{What $f$ can look like}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_u2_shapes.pdf}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.3em}
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item \textbf{A straight line.} One slope, and you can say it out loud in units.
  \item \textbf{A curve.} One extra term, still readable.
  \item \textbf{A tree.} A step function, which is a set of if-then rules.
  \item \textbf{A forest.} An average of hundreds of trees, chasing every bump here.
\end{itemize}
\centering\small
Same 392 cars, same predictor, four families. Only $f$ changed.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two kinds of thing get called a model}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{A probability model}\\[2pt]
Says where the data came from. It writes down a distribution:
\[ y \sim \text{Normal}(b_0 + b_1 x,\; \sigma^2) \]
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{An algorithmic model}\\[2pt]
Says nothing about where the data came from. It gives you a rule that turns inputs into a
prediction, tuned until the predictions are good.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.4em}
\begin{principle}{why the split matters}
A model that names a distribution has already said what \emph{other} datasets like yours would
have looked like, so it can tell you how much its own numbers would move. A model that only
produces predictions cannot. To learn how much it would move, you have to hold data back and
watch it miss.
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Start with the type of $y$}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{fig_u2_ytypes.pdf}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.2em}
\centering\small
This is the one part of the choice that is not a judgment call, and it removes most of the menu
before you have thought about anything else.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What each type of $y$ points to}
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{L{0.15\textwidth} L{0.21\textwidth} L{0.26\textwidth} L{0.24\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{$y$ is} & \textbf{Example} & \textbf{Probability model} & \textbf{Algorithmic} \\
\midrule
a number & mpg, rent, revenue & linear regression & trees, forests, boosting \\
yes or no & churn, default, click & logistic regression & classification trees and forests \\
a count or rate & tickets per hour & Poisson, negative binomial & boosting on counts \\
a category & which of four plans & multinomial logistic & classification models \\
time to an event & months until churn & survival models & specialized versions \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{trap}{forcing the wrong type}
Run an ordinary linear regression on a yes/no outcome and it will produce numbers. Some will be
predicted probabilities above 1 or below 0, which should tell you what happened.
\end{trap}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Linear regression}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_u2_m_linear.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}\small
\[ y \sim \text{Normal}(b_0 + b_1x,\; \sigma^2) \]
\begin{itemize}
  \item $y$ is a \textbf{number}.
  \item $f$ is a straight line, so one slope describes the whole relationship.
  \item You can read $b_1$ out loud in units and defend it.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Costs you:} the world has to be roughly straight, or you have to bend it yourself.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Logistic regression}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_u2_m_logistic.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}\small
\[ y \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p(x)) \]
\begin{itemize}
  \item $y$ is \textbf{yes or no}. The output is a probability, never a label.
  \item $f$ is a straight line on the log-odds, which becomes an S-curve on the probability.
  \item Predictions are trapped between 0 and 1 by construction.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Costs you:} somebody still has to pick the cutoff for acting.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Poisson regression}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_u2_m_poisson.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}\small
\[ y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda(x)) \]
\begin{itemize}
  \item $y$ is a \textbf{count}: bikes rented in an hour, tickets, complaints.
  \item $f$ works on the log of the rate, so predictions can never go negative.
  \item Built in: counts are more variable when the rate is higher.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Costs you:} it assumes the variance equals the mean, which real counts often exceed.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Decision tree}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{fig_u2_m_tree.pdf}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.3em}
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item Splits the data one variable at a time and predicts the average inside each box.
  \item $f$ is a \textbf{step function}, and the same thing written out is a set of if-then rules
        a human can follow.
  \item No distribution is named anywhere, so there is nothing here to test.
\end{itemize}
\centering\small \textbf{Costs you:} move a few rows and the splits can land somewhere else entirely.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Random forest}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_u2_m_forest.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item Grow hundreds of trees on resampled data, then average them.
  \item Each single tree is unstable. Averaging cancels most of that out, which is the whole
        idea.
  \item Solid predictions with almost no tuning.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Costs you:} there is no line to read any more, and no coefficient to report.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Gradient boosting}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_u2_m_boost.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item Trees added one at a time, each one fitting what the previous ones got wrong.
  \item One tree is a crude step. Ten is closer. Two hundred traces the shape.
  \item Usually the most accurate thing you can fit to a table of numbers.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Costs you:} several hyperparameters that matter, and it will overfit if you let it.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{$k$-nearest neighbours}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_u2_m_knn.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item To predict a new case, find the $k$ most similar past cases and average them.
  \item There is no fitted equation at all. The training data \emph{is} the model.
  \item $k$ sets the smoothness: 3 is jumpy, 40 is smooth.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Costs you:} it degrades badly once you have many columns, and it stores everything.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Lasso and ridge}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_u2_m_lasso.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item A linear regression with a penalty on large coefficients.
  \item Raise the penalty and coefficients slide toward zero. The lasso sends most of them to
        exactly zero, which is how it picks a shortlist.
  \item Useful when you have far more columns than you can defend.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Costs you:} every coefficient is now deliberately biased, so they are not effects.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The menu, on one page}
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{L{0.22\textwidth} L{0.14\textwidth} L{0.23\textwidth} L{0.25\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Names a distribution} & \textbf{$f$ is} & \textbf{Reach for it when} \\
\midrule
Linear regression & yes & a straight line & you must defend a number \\
Logistic regression & yes & an S-curve on a probability & $y$ is yes or no and someone reads it \\
Poisson regression & yes & a curve on a rate & $y$ is a count \\
Decision tree & no & a step function & you want followable rules \\
Random forest & no & an average of trees & prediction matters, reading does not \\
Gradient boosting & no & a sum of small trees & you want the best accuracy available \\
$k$-nearest neighbours & no & a local average & few columns, and similarity is meaningful \\
Lasso and ridge & partly & a penalized line & too many columns to defend \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\section{What you want from it}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{Five jobs people hand a model}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Predict.} Give me a number for this new case.
  \item \textbf{Explain.} Does $X$ actually move $Y$, and by how much?
  \item \textbf{Screen.} Out of these 200 columns, which few are worth tracking?
  \item \textbf{Decide.} Given what it costs to be wrong each way, what should we do?
  \item \textbf{Deploy.} Run this every night for two years, and be able to explain any single
        output to someone who is unhappy about it.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{principle}{say which one out loud first}
A model that is excellent at the first can be useless at the second, and the choice that makes
the fifth easy usually costs you accuracy on the first.
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Job 1: predict}
\textbf{The ask.} A listings site wants an estimated rent on every apartment page.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What matters:} error on apartments the model has never seen. Nothing else.
  \item \textbf{Reach for:} boosting or a forest when you have plenty of rows, a regression when
        rows are scarce.
  \item \textbf{Nobody will ever read a coefficient}, so nothing constrains you except accuracy
        and whatever you need to keep it running.
\end{itemize}
\begin{trap}{judging it on the wrong data}
A model that predicts the rows it was fitted to is not predicting. It is remembering. The only
number that counts here comes from data the model never saw.
\end{trap}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Job 2: explain}
\textbf{The ask.} A city council wants to know whether apartment size drives rent, and will write
policy from your answer.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What matters:} one coefficient, its units, and an honest range around it.
  \item \textbf{Reach for:} linear or logistic regression. Only a probability model gives you a
        number you can defend.
  \item Accuracy is almost beside the point. A model that predicts worse but reports a
        trustworthy slope wins this job outright.
\end{itemize}
\begin{trap}{the forest cannot do this one}
Feature importance is not an effect. It has no units, no direction, and it splits credit between
correlated columns close to arbitrarily.
\end{trap}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Job 3: screen}
\textbf{The ask.} A hospital records 60 things at intake and wants to know which are worth
continuing to collect.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What matters:} getting from 60 down to a handful, without hand-picking.
  \item \textbf{Reach for:} the lasso. Turn the penalty up and most coefficients go to exactly
        zero, leaving a shortlist.
  \item Report it as \textbf{a shortlist}, which is what it is.
\end{itemize}
\begin{trap}{the survivors are not effects}
They were shrunk on purpose, and they were chosen by looking at the data. If you need defensible
numbers, refit an ordinary regression on the survivors and say that you screened first.
\end{trap}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Job 4: decide}
\textbf{The ask.} A bank has a model that outputs a default probability. Now what?
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What matters:} a probability you can trust, plus what each kind of mistake costs.
  \item \textbf{Reach for:} anything that outputs a calibrated probability. The model is half the
        problem.
  \item The other half is the \textbf{threshold}, and that is a business decision, not a
        statistical one.
\end{itemize}
\begin{principle}{the costs are rarely symmetric}
Approving a bad loan and rejecting a good customer do not cost the same amount, so 0.5 is almost
never the right cutoff. Unit 11 does this properly.
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Job 5: deploy}
\textbf{The ask.} This runs every night for two years and someone will call about a specific
output.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{What matters:} stability, few moving parts, and a story for any single
        prediction.
  \item \textbf{Reach for:} the simplest model that clears the accuracy bar you actually need.
  \item A model with six tuned hyperparameters is six things that can drift when the data does.
\end{itemize}
\begin{practice}{a strong thing to say}
``Boosting was two points better in testing. I shipped the regression, because we have to explain
rejections to customers and I would rather be able to.''
\end{practice}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The five jobs, side by side}
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{L{0.11\textwidth} L{0.29\textwidth} L{0.22\textwidth} L{0.23\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Job} & \textbf{Reach for} & \textbf{Judged by} & \textbf{Costs you} \\
\midrule
Predict & boosting or a forest, regression if rows are scarce & out-of-sample error
& coefficients nobody can read \\
Explain & linear or logistic regression & the interval on one coefficient
& accuracy you could have had \\
Screen & the lasso & does the shortlist hold up & valid inference on the survivors \\
Decide & anything calibrated, plus a cost table & expected cost, not accuracy
& work nobody asked for \\
Deploy & the simplest thing that clears the bar & does it still work in a year
& the last few points of accuracy \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Your turn \#1: which model, and why}
\begin{yourturn}{name the type of $y$ first, then the family}
\begin{enumerate}\small
  \item A hospital wants to know which of 60 measurements taken at intake are worth continuing to
        collect.
  \item A subscription business wants, for each customer, the chance they cancel next month.
  \item A city wants to know whether a new bus lane reduced average commute time.
  \item A support team wants to know how many tickets will arrive each hour tomorrow.
\end{enumerate}
\end{yourturn}
Two of these have the same type of $y$ and call for different models anyway. Find them.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Your turn \#1: answer}
\small
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Screening.} The lasso. The job is cutting 60 down to a handful, and that is what
        a penalty is for. Report a shortlist, not effects.
  \item \textbf{Yes or no.} Logistic regression, or boosting if nobody needs to read it. The ask
        is a \emph{probability}, and somebody still chooses the cutoff for acting on it.
  \item \textbf{A number, but the real problem is causal.} Linear regression with the bus lane as
        the predictor of interest. Whether the lane was the only thing that changed is Unit 6.
  \item \textbf{A count.} Poisson regression. Force hourly tickets into an ordinary linear model
        and it will happily predict negative tickets at 3 a.m.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{principle}{}
Items 2 and 3 share nothing. Items 1 and 3 could both end up as a regression, for completely
different reasons.
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\section{What you do with it}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{The five things you do to any model}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Fit it.} Estimate the parts the data decides.
  \item \textbf{Tune it.} Set the parts the data does not decide.
  \item \textbf{Predict with it.} Push a new case through.
  \item \textbf{Say how wrong it might be.} Attach a range to that prediction.
  \item \textbf{Judge it against an alternative.} A number alone means nothing.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{principle}{the list does not change, the tools do}
Every family on the menu goes through all five. What differs is which tool does each step, and
whether the model can supply it for you or you have to get it from held-out data.
\end{principle}
\centering\small
The next five slides walk one regression tree through all of it, on the 392 cars.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 1: fit it}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{fig_u2_m_tree.pdf}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.3em}
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item Fitting a tree means finding the \textbf{splits}: which variable, and at what cutoff.
  \item Here it chose weight at 2764, then 2217 and 3658, giving four groups with average mpg of
        33, 26, 20, and 15.
  \item Those numbers are what the data decided. Nobody chose them.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 2: tune it}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
\centering\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_u2_mse.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}\small
\textbf{Depth} is not estimated. You choose it, and the data cannot choose it for you by fitting.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Training error falls forever. By depth 10 it is near zero.
  \item Honest error bottoms out at \textbf{depth 4} and then climbs.
\end{itemize}
So depth 4, chosen on folds fixed before looking.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 3: predict with it}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fit the depth-4 tree on 294 cars, hold back 98 it never sees.
  \item Push one held-out car through: it lands in a leaf, and the prediction is that leaf's
        average.
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}\large
predicted mpg $= 22.8$
\end{center}
\begin{principle}{a tree predicts by lookup}
There is no equation to evaluate. The car falls through a sequence of yes/no questions and comes
out in a box, and the answer is the average of the training cars already in that box.
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 4: say how wrong it might be}
The tree names no distribution, so it cannot hand you a range. Get one from how badly it missed
on the 98 cars it never saw.
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item Collect those 98 held-out errors, $y_i - \hat{y}_i$.
  \item The 5th and 95th percentiles are $-4.6$ and $+7.6$ mpg.
  \item Attach them to the prediction.
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}\large
$22.8 - 4.6 \;=\; 18.2$ \qquad to \qquad $22.8 + 7.6 \;=\; 30.4$ mpg
\end{center}
\centering\small
That car's actual mpg was \textbf{23.0}, comfortably inside. A probability model would have
produced this range from its own assumptions instead.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 5: judge it against an alternative}
Same six predictors, same five folds, three candidates.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{L{0.28\textwidth} L{0.22\textwidth} L{0.22\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Training MSE} & \textbf{Cross-validated MSE} \\
\midrule
Linear regression & 11.59 & 12.23 \\
Tree, depth 4     & \phantom{0}6.74 & \textbf{11.63} \\
Tree, depth 10    & \phantom{0}0.44 & 13.19 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{trap}{the middle column is a trap}
Ranked on training error, the depth-10 tree wins by a mile. On data it did not see, it is the
worst of the three, and worse than a straight line.
\end{trap}
\end{frame}

\section{Measuring}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{What ``error'' means, exactly}
For a numeric outcome, the usual measure is \textbf{mean squared error}:
\[
\text{MSE} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - \hat{y}_i\bigr)^2
\qquad\qquad
\text{RMSE} \;=\; \sqrt{\text{MSE}}
\]
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item Squaring makes big misses count much more than small ones. A miss of 4 counts sixteen
        times a miss of 1.
  \item MSE is in squared units, which nobody can picture. RMSE is back in the units of $y$.
  \item Our depth-4 tree: MSE $= 11.63$, so RMSE $= 3.41$ mpg. That is the number to quote.
\end{itemize}
\begin{principle}{the measure is a choice}
Squared error is a decision that one big miss is worse than several small ones. If that is not
true for your problem, use absolute error instead, and say so.
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two ways to get an honest number}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Hold out once}\\[3pt]
Split the rows, fit on most, score on the rest. Simple, and the score depends on which rows
happened to be held out.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Cross-validate}\\[3pt]
Split into $k$ folds. Fit on $k-1$, score on the one left out, rotate, average. Every row gets
scored exactly once, by a model that never saw it.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{principle}{same rule either way}
Anything you learned from the data counts as fitting. Choosing depth, choosing which columns to
keep, even choosing the scaling. If it happened before scoring, it has to happen inside the fold.
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{AIC and BIC: paying for parameters}
A \textbf{probability model} can report how likely your data was under the fitted model. Call
that the likelihood $L$. Bigger is better, but adding parameters always makes it bigger, so a
raw comparison always picks the biggest model.
\[
\text{AIC} \;=\; 2k - 2\ln L
\qquad\qquad
\text{BIC} \;=\; k\ln n - 2\ln L
\]
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item $k$ is how many parameters you estimated, $n$ is how many rows you have.
  \item The second term rewards fit. The first charges you for the parameters that bought it.
  \item \textbf{Lower is better.} The raw value means nothing on its own, only differences do.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{AIC, worked on the cars}
$n = 392$. Three nested models, each fit to the same rows.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{L{0.30\textwidth} L{0.08\textwidth} L{0.14\textwidth} L{0.30\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{$k$} & \textbf{$\ln L$} & \textbf{AIC $= 2k - 2\ln L$} \\
\midrule
mpg on weight & 2 & $-1130.0$ & $4 + 2260.0 = 2264.0$ \\
\quad + horsepower & 3 & $-1121.0$ & $6 + 2242.0 = 2248.0$ \\
\quad + model year & 4 & $-1037.4$ & $8 + 2074.8 = 2082.8$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item Horsepower buys 16 points of AIC, and model year buys another 165. Both are worth their
        one parameter.
  \item BIC charges $\ln(392) = 5.97$ per parameter instead of 2, so it prefers smaller models,
        and the gap widens as $n$ grows.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which comparison to use}
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{L{0.26\textwidth} L{0.30\textwidth} L{0.32\textwidth}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{AIC and BIC} & \textbf{Out-of-sample error} \\
\midrule
Needs a likelihood & yes, so probability models only & no, works on anything \\
Data used & all of it, once & held out, or rotated in folds \\
Cost & one fit per model & $k$ fits per model \\
Compares across families & no & yes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{trap}{comparing numbers that are not comparable}
An AIC of 2082 and a cross-validated MSE of 11.63 are different quantities on different scales,
and neither one is ``better'' than the other. Pick one method and use it for every candidate, on
identical rows.
\end{trap}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The two-model habit}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fit the simplest defensible model and a flexible one. Both, every time.
  \item If they agree, report the simple one. Easier to defend, cheaper to maintain, less likely
        to break when the data shifts.
  \item If they disagree, you have learned something specific: there is structure the simple
        model is missing. Go find out what it is.
\end{itemize}
\begin{practice}{a strong thing to say}
``The forest beat the regression by four points, so I looked for what it was using, found the
threshold at 30 days, added that to the regression, and it caught up. I shipped the regression.''
\end{practice}
\end{frame}

\begin{yourturn}{name the family and the reason}

  \item 60 trial patients, one treatment variable, and the question is whether the drug works.
  \item 25{,}000 sensor readings where you know the relationship bends but not how.
  \item A model that has run nightly for a year suddenly gets worse. Which of the five steps do

\end{enumerate}
\end{yourturn}

\begin{frame}{Your turn \#2: answer}
\small
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A $t$-test, or a regression with the treatment indicator.} With $n=60$ and one
        variable of interest, a flexible model would spend your data estimating things nobody
        asked about. Randomization is doing the work here, not the model.
  \item \textbf{Both.} A flexible fit to see the shape, then name it and put it in a regression
        you can explain. That is the two-model habit doing its job.
  \item \textbf{None of them first. Check whether the data still looks like the training data.}
        Nothing about the model changed, so the thing that changed is outside it. Re-tuning a
        model against a shifted world just fits the new noise.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section{LLM check}
% ======================================================================
\begin{frame}{LLM check: mistake or not?}
\begin{llmcheck}{you asked for help choosing a model}
``With 200 patients and 45 predictors I'd recommend a random forest. It handles nonlinearity and
interactions automatically and gives you feature importances, so you can report the top
predictors as the key risk factors and their $p$-values.''
\end{llmcheck}
\vspace{0.2em}
\centering\small Three separate problems. Find them before the next slide.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{LLM check: answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The ratio.} 200 rows against 45 columns is the corner where flexible models fit
        well and predict badly. A penalized regression is the honest choice.
  \item \textbf{``Automatically'' costs something.} Discovering interactions means spending data
        to find them. With 200 rows you cannot afford the search.
  \item \textbf{A forest has no $p$-values.} It names no distribution, so the quantity being
        asked for does not exist. And an importance score ranks correlated columns close to
        arbitrarily, so it is not an effect either.
\end{itemize}
\begin{principle}{}
The answer is not wrong about what a forest does. It is wrong about whether this dataset can pay
for one, and about what the output means.
\end{principle}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Choosing a model, in order}
\small
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Name the job.} Predict, explain, screen, decide, or deploy.
  \item \textbf{Name the type of $y$.} A number, a yes or no, a count, a category. Not a judgment
        call, and it removes most of the menu.
  \item \textbf{Decide whether you need parameters that mean something.} If yes, you are in the
        probability column and that is the end of the discussion.
  \item \textbf{Count what you have against what you are estimating.} Rows, columns, and noise.
  \item \textbf{Fit the simple one first.} It is your baseline and often your answer.
  \item \textbf{Fit a flexible one on the same folds}, compare with one method, and take the
        simpler model when they are close.
  \item \textbf{Report on data you touched once}, and say what else you tried.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The traps, all in one place}
\begin{trap}{the ones that bite most often}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Choosing the algorithm before naming the job.
  \item Forcing a linear model onto a count or a yes/no outcome.
  \item Judging a model on the rows it was fitted to.
  \item Asking an algorithmic model for a $p$-value or an AIC.
  \item Reading a penalized or shrunk coefficient as an effect.
  \item Comparing an AIC against a cross-validated error.
  \item Tuning a hyperparameter until the answer looks right.
\end{itemize}
\end{trap}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What you should be able to do with software}
\small
Nobody is asking you to memorize syntax. You are expected to know which procedure the question
calls for, what to hand it, and what the output means.

\end{frame}
