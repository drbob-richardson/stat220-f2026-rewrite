# The causal unit, retired

This was **Unit 5: Causal Claims and Where the Data Came From**, a merge of the
old Causal Thinking and Where the Data Comes From decks. It is no longer in the
course.

Dropped 2026-10-06. The reasoning, in Dr. Richardson's words after teaching the
first weeks: the course had gone too hard into its statistical-thinking goals and
left the practical and applied side too far behind, and causality was the
casualty. Unit 5 is now **When a Line Is Not Enough**, which is remedial
measures, transformations, and decision trees.

## What is here

- `Unit_05_Causal_and_Data_Provenance.tex` and its PDF, 51 pages
- `Code_Unit05_Causal_and_Provenance.ipynb`, the code companion
- `Stat_220_HW_Unit05_Causal_and_Provenance.ipynb`, the homework
- `practice_05_causal.py` and `Practice_Causal.pdf`, ten practice questions

Nothing here was ever released to students.

## What survives elsewhere, and where

Dropping the unit does not remove causal caution from the course. It lives in the
units that do the modelling:

- **Unit 3** has the coefficient that changes its mind, confounding, the
  ice-cream-and-drowning example, Simpson's paradox, and the frame on
  correlation and causation stated precisely. Its LLM check is an unearned
  causal claim.
- **Unit 4** ends on a model that predicts well being the wrong basis for a
  decision about what to change, and its last multiple-choice question is about
  an observational coefficient.
- **The midterms** both turn on a comparison that misleads until you adjust, and
  both ask for a written answer that stops short of a causal promise.

## What was lost

The things only this unit covered, in case any should be folded in later:

- randomized experiments and what random assignment buys
- DAGs, colliders, and the formal collider example
- difference in differences
- survivorship and non-response bias as named ideas
- the data provenance material: how a dataset came to exist, who is missing
  from it, and what that does to a conclusion

The selection-bias idea has the strongest claim to a home somewhere else, since
it is the one students meet in the wild most often.
